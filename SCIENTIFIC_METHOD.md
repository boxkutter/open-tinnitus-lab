# Open Tinnitus Lab — Scientific Method

## Purpose

This document defines how Open Tinnitus Lab evaluates evidence, develops hypotheses and attempts to produce new scientific knowledge.

The central objective is to prevent the project from becoming an AI-generated collection of plausible but unverified explanations.

---

# 1. Scientific hierarchy

The project uses the following hierarchy:

```text
Observation
    ↓
Finding
    ↓
Interpretation
    ↓
Hypothesis
    ↓
Prediction
    ↓
Test
    ↓
Replication
    ↓
Scientific conclusion
```

Each stage has a different evidentiary standard.

---

# 2. Observation

An observation is something directly measured, recorded or reported.

Examples:

* a change in neural activity
* a hearing threshold
* a change in tinnitus intensity
* a biochemical measurement
* an imaging finding

An observation does not by itself establish its cause.

---

# 3. Finding

A finding is an observation resulting from an appropriate analysis or experiment.

The project should record:

* what was measured
* in whom or what it was measured
* how it was measured
* sample size
* statistical method
* uncertainty
* limitations

---

# 4. Interpretation

An interpretation explains what a finding might mean.

For example:

> Increased neural activity in auditory cortex may indicate altered central auditory processing.

This is an interpretation, not proof that altered auditory processing causes tinnitus.

---

# 5. Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that makes testable predictions.

A useful hypothesis should answer:

* What causes what?
* Under what conditions?
* In which population?
* By what mechanism?
* What should we observe if the hypothesis is correct?
* What observation would falsify it?

---

# 6. Predictions

Every major hypothesis should produce explicit predictions.

For example:

```text
HYPOTHESIS

A particular biological mechanism contributes causally
to tinnitus in a defined subgroup.

PREDICTION

Individuals in this subgroup should demonstrate
measurement X under condition Y.

FALSIFICATION

If repeated well-controlled studies fail to observe X,
the hypothesis should be weakened or rejected.
```

---

# 7. Correlation is not causation

The project must explicitly distinguish:

* correlation
* association
* prediction
* mediation
* causation

Evidence that two variables occur together does not establish that one causes the other.

Where causal claims are made, the project should seek appropriate evidence such as:

* longitudinal studies
* natural experiments
* intervention studies
* randomized controlled trials
* mechanistic experiments
* converging evidence from independent methodologies

---

# 8. Evidence quality

Evidence should be assessed using multiple dimensions.

Potential dimensions include:

### Methodological quality

How well was the study designed?

### Sample quality

How appropriate and representative was the population?

### Measurement quality

How reliable and valid were the measurements?

### Statistical quality

Were the statistical methods appropriate?

### Replication

Has the finding been independently reproduced?

### Consistency

Do other studies report similar results?

### Biological plausibility

Is there a credible mechanism?

### Alternative explanations

Could another mechanism explain the observation?

### Publication bias

Could the apparent evidence be affected by selective publication?

---

# 9. Contradictory evidence

Contradictory evidence must be actively sought.

For every important hypothesis the project should ask:

> What evidence would contradict this hypothesis?

and:

> Does such evidence already exist?

Contradictory studies must not automatically be treated as inferior simply because they disagree with the majority.

Differences in:

* population
* tinnitus subtype
* methodology
* measurement
* statistical power
* timing
* disease duration

must be investigated before conclusions are drawn.

---

# 10. Replication

Replication is especially important for claims generated or identified by AI.

A finding should ideally be reproduced using:

* an independent dataset
* an independent research group
* a different analytical implementation
* a different experimental methodology

depending on the nature of the finding.

---

# 11. AI-generated hypotheses

AI may generate hypotheses.

An AI-generated hypothesis has no special evidentiary status.

It should enter the same scientific process as a human-generated hypothesis:

```text
AI hypothesis
     ↓
Literature review
     ↓
Existing evidence
     ↓
Contradictory evidence
     ↓
Testable prediction
     ↓
Independent test
```

---

# 12. AI hallucination control

AI systems may invent:

* papers
* citations
* experimental results
* mechanisms
* statistics
* quotations
* biological explanations

Therefore:

**No scientific claim should be accepted solely because an AI system produced it.**

Primary sources should be checked whenever practical.

DOIs, PubMed identifiers and other persistent identifiers should be recorded where available.

---

# 13. Source hierarchy

When possible, evidence should be traced toward the original source.

A general preference is:

1. Primary peer-reviewed research
2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses
3. Major scientific consensus/guideline documents
4. High-quality review articles
5. Preprints
6. Secondary reporting
7. Expert commentary
8. Unverified online claims

Lower-level sources may be useful for discovery but should not silently become evidence.

---

# 14. Retractions and corrections

Retracted papers must never be treated as valid supporting evidence.

Corrected papers should reference the correction.

The database should preserve the historical record while clearly identifying the current status of the publication.

---

# 15. Research gaps

A research gap is not simply a topic that has few papers.

A meaningful research gap may be:

* an unresolved contradiction
* an untested prediction
* a missing measurement
* a missing population
* a missing longitudinal study
* a missing causal experiment
* an unexplained observation
* a mechanism with competing explanations

These gaps are potential sources of new research questions.

---

# 16. Hypothesis scoring

The project may eventually score hypotheses.

Any scoring system must remain transparent.

Possible dimensions include:

* quality of supporting evidence
* quantity of supporting evidence
* replication
* contradictory evidence
* predictive power
* biological plausibility
* explanatory coverage
* testability

Scores are decision-support tools, not measures of scientific truth.

---

# 17. Discovery standard

The project should use a high standard before describing something as a discovery.

A potential discovery should ideally involve:

1. A previously unknown observation, relationship, mechanism or construct.
2. A reproducible method for demonstrating it.
3. Evidence that alternative explanations have been considered.
4. Independent verification where practical.
5. Appropriate scientific review.

AI novelty is not sufficient.

---

# 18. Final principle

The laboratory should continuously ask:

> **What would change our mind?**

If the answer is "nothing", the proposition is not being treated scientifically.
